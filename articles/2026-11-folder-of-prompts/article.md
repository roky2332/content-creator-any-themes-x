## Part 1. How to build a woman who doesn't exist

[ILLUSTRATION: 01-folder-anatomy.png]

### Step 1. A face built to a formula

The course doesn't tell you to make someone beautiful. It tells you to make someone specific

There are, according to the hosts, two ends of a spectrum. On one end: "a round face, big forehead, big eyes, big lips, a small chin, a very submissive feminine look"

Their advice for beginners is blunt

"Your best bet is to build the most feminine, submissive looking model you possibly can" [09:22]

You pull two or three reference faces from Pinterest. You generate a "master face reference" and reuse it forever, so she looks identical in every single image. Use a different reference, they warn, and "she's going to look different in every single [...] image"

The consistency is the product

### Step 2. Erase the proof

This is the step that should stop you

The images are generated with Nano Banana Pro, Google's image model. Then they're "refined" in a second tool that will do what the first one blocks: "put them in bikinis and stuff. Stuff that Nano Banana Pro pretty much blocks" [03:02]

Then every image goes through a metadata remover

The course is clear about what that tool is for. "Removing the metadata and the synth ID" [03:22]

SynthID is Google DeepMind's invisible watermark. It's embedded in the pixels of AI images so that anyone can later check whether a picture was made by AI

It exists for exactly one reason: so people can tell what's real

The hosts say they've "tried and tested several different metadata removers" over the last 12 months to find the one that strips it cleanly

### Step 3. Borrow a real woman's body

Static photos aren't enough anymore. The course says image-only reels are "completely done and dusted". The meta now is video

So they show you how to make it

"Real girl video replication. So first things first, over here we have Sophie Rain's video. We are taking the first frame, taking it over, swapping it with our Nano Banana model's face" [18:57]

Sophie Rain is a real creator. A real woman filmed that clip on a beach. In the course, her movement becomes someone else's content

Their philosophy on originality is spelled out a few minutes later: "If it's gone viral for someone else, it will go viral for you" [28:12]

### Step 4. Give her a life that belongs to other people

An AI model that only posts selfies looks like a bot. So the course teaches you to give her a life

The trick: fill her stories with real photos taken from real people

"Whether you're taking it from people's Instagrams, you're taking it from Google, Pinterest" [28:30]

Football boots on a pitch. A sauna. A healthy lunch. Anything "non identifiable to the model", as long as the skin tone matches

"They're never going to put two and two together" [28:56]

The goal is said out loud: "It is going to make your model look like a real person. She isn't just making reels… She's actually living a real life just like any real girl you'd follow on Instagram" [29:12]

### Step 5. Point the traffic at a paywall

Then comes the funnel

Instagram is the "non-negotiable primary source of traffic". Facebook, TikTok and Snapchat repost the same content. Everything leads to a landing page, then to Telegram, then to a paid page on Fanvue

[ILLUSTRATION: 02-funnel.png]

On Telegram the content gets "a level higher". The caption on a teaser photo: "If you message me on Fanvue I'll send you the full pic"

And once the fan messages, "your chatters get to work"

## Part 5. The twist: the rules say the opposite

Here's what the course doesn't mention

Fanvue, the platform at the end of this funnel, does allow AI creators. But its Community Guidelines say all AI-generated media "must include a clear and prominent disclosure", and its help center tells fans that AI creators are marked with an AI tag

So the platform's position is simple: fans should know

The course's position is just as simple: "You don't want them to be aware"

One side built a label. The other side built a 73-minute lesson on making sure nobody reads it

## Part 7. How to tell if she's real

[ILLUSTRATION: 06-how-to-spot.png]

Every sign below comes straight from the course. It's their checklist, reversed

**1. The same 3 to 5 places.** "The top creators reuse the same three to five locations consistently. This builds realism" [34:37]. If every photo is the same bedroom, the same car and the same café, ask why

**2. A face that never changes.** Real people look different in bad light, at bad angles, on bad days. A model built on one "master face reference" never does

**3. Stories without a face.** Feet, nails, a sauna, football boots, a plate of food. These are exactly the "non identifiable" photos the course tells you to take from other people's accounts

**4. Reels you've already seen.** If her video copies a viral clip beat for beat, the course literally calls that a "proven format"

**5. Reverse image search her stories.** Borrowed photos usually have an original owner

**6. Check for the AI label.** On platforms like Fanvue, AI creators are supposed to be tagged. If the label is missing and she insists she's real, that tells you something

**7. She always "just woke up", always "bored and lonely tonight".** The course's own example of a call to action: "I'm bored and lonely tonight. Someone help me"
